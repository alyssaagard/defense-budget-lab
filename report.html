1. What This Project Is

The Defense Budget Lab is an interactive reading of two Congressional Research Service reports, FY2026 Defense Budget: Funding for Selected Weapon Systems (R48860) and its FY2027 successor (R49023), joined line by line across the two fiscal years and set beside a two year model of military pay and compensation. It sets out to show what the request for selected weapon systems actually contains, what Congress has done to it, what changed from one year to the next once the two reports are made comparable, and what any proposed change would cost in terms a reader can check for themselves.

The project has one source of truth and one delivered page. The source of truth is a workbook of twenty-seven sheets carrying more than fifteen thousand live formulas. Every computed cell in it is a formula rather than a pasted result, which costs more to build but means the arithmetic can be audited inside the file itself. The page is generated from that workbook by a Python program whose only dependency is openpyxl. It is a single self-contained HTML file of about one megabyte that runs from a local file and makes no network request of any kind. The workbook travels inside the page and can be saved from it, so a reader who is handed only the page still holds the source.

The distinguishing feature of the project is not its subject. Budget tables are published every year and summarized widely. The distinguishing feature is the insistence that every figure trace to a page: that each line carry the CRS table, page, and footnote it came from; that a blank, meaning a figure CRS did not print, never be displayed as a zero; that where CRS's own narrative and CRS's own tables disagree, both figures be kept and the gap named; and that the generator refuse to write a page when anything in the workbook has moved, broken, or stopped reconciling. Where the sources contradict one another, the page says so, and that admission is treated as part of the finding rather than as a defect to be smoothed away.

2. How the Project Came About

2.1 The starting point

The project began out of pure curiosity, and the object of that curiosity was the reports themselves. The CRS series on funding for selected weapon systems is one of the most useful public references on what Congress does with the money that buys weapons. For each program it sets the Department of Defense request beside the marks of the armed services and appropriations committees and, once they exist, the enacted amounts. It is also published as a set of tables inside a PDF. A reader can look a program up, but cannot query the tables, compare them across years, total them under a different assumption, or test whether a claim made about them is in proportion.

The project also follows from how I approach research more generally. Whether the subject is a program, the training that carries a doctrine into the force, or the development of a weapon, the question I return to is whether it can realistically be implemented. That question eventually comes down to what is funded, in what amount, over what period, and through which mechanism. A budget line is where an intention becomes a commitment that can be counted, which makes a budget table a primary source in the historian's sense: a document whose meaning depends on reading exactly what it says, what it leaves blank, and how its categories were drawn. The first step was therefore not automation. The tables were first transcribed by hand to learn their structure, because the structure of a table, meaning which columns exist, which cells are empty, and what each footnote qualifies, determines what can defensibly be said about the numbers inside it.

2.2 From one fiscal year to two

The first version covered FY2026 alone: ninety-seven lines from R48860 across eight categories, the reconciliation appendix, the legislative timeline, and a module for pricing a military pay raise. When CRS published the FY2027 edition, the natural next question was what had changed. That question proved harder than it looks, because the two reports do not share a vocabulary.

R48860 separates a base request from a reconciliation request. FY2026 was the first year Congress used budget reconciliation to add money to the Department of Defense, and the reconciliation figures sit in a separate appendix table built after P.L. 119-21 had passed. R49023 prints a discretionary request and a mandatory request side by side inside its category tables, with no reconciliation law behind the mandatory column. Its appendix is not a spend plan at all: Appendix Table A-1 lists three administration initiatives. Program names also drift between editions. A frigate loses its class name, and Terminal High Altitude Area Defense becomes Terminal High Altitude Air Defense.

A comparison built by matching column headings would therefore compare unlike things. The FY2027 extension was built around an explicit crosswalk instead, one that states which pairs of columns are comparable before any figure is compared and records the basis for every match between the two years.

2.3 The observation that shaped the design

One observation did more than any other to shape the project. In a CRS table, an empty cell means that CRS printed no figure on that line. It does not mean zero. The distinction sounds pedantic until it is lost.

R49023 lists four programs that also appear in R48860 without printing any FY2027 request against them: C-40 Fleet Expansion, E-7A Wedgetail, M10 Booker, and the Sea-Launched Cruise Missile-Nuclear. Opened in Excel, the crosswalk as first built would have shown each of those programs as cut by the whole of its FY2026 request, because a bare reference to an empty cell on another sheet returns zero in Excel, though not in LibreOffice, where the fault stayed invisible. The E-7A Wedgetail, which carried $0.20 billion in FY2026, would have appeared to be eliminated, while the Senate Armed Services Committee column in the same report proposes $1.50 billion more for it than the request. A blank read as a zero would have manufactured a program termination that no document records.

The intuitive treatment of an empty cell and the correct one point in opposite directions, and that gap is precisely what the project exists to close. The observation became a governing rule, enforced in the workbook, in the generator, and on the page: a program CRS did not price is not a program cut to zero.

3. The Questions, the Principle, and the Design

3.1 The questions

The lab is built to answer five questions of the two reports.

  • What does the request for selected weapon systems contain, category by category, and how much of it depends on mandatory money rather than on annual appropriations?

  • What have the committees done to it, and do their aggregate positions, as CRS summarizes them, agree with the line level tables CRS prints?

  • What changed between FY2026 and FY2027 on the programs that can properly be compared, and which programs appear in only one of the two years?

  • What would a proposed change cost, where would the money come from, and how large is it in proportion to its own line, its category, and the force?

  • What would discretionary appropriations have to carry to sustain the FY2026 program once the one-time reconciliation money has been spent?

3.2 The governing principle

A budget figure is only as reliable as its path back to the page on which it was printed. Every design decision in the project follows from that sentence. Totals are computed from lines and set beside the totals CRS states, never substituted for them. Where the two disagree by more than rounding, both figures are kept and the disagreement is explained. No figure on the page is recalculated in the browser; the page reads what the workbook computed, and the workbook computes it with formulas a reader can inspect. Nothing is adjusted to make a total come out.

3.3 Why this is a reading and not a forecast

The page contains two tools that look forward, the scenario composer and the reconciliation cliff projection, and a claim tester that invites hypotheticals. None of them predicts anything. Each takes a stated assumption, applies it to published figures, and reports the arithmetic. The projection, for example, holds the FY2026 program constant and asks what discretionary appropriations would have to carry from FY2027 onward to sustain it. It does not estimate what Congress will do, and it says so on its face.

The distinction matters because budget analysis is frequently presented with a confidence its method cannot support. A priced assumption tells a reader what follows if the assumption holds; a forecast claims to know whether it will. The terminology is used consistently throughout: these are readings and priced assumptions, not forecasts.

3.4 The two reports

  • R48860, FY2026 Defense Budget: Funding for Selected Weapon Systems, version 7, updated 2 July 2026. Ninety-seven lines in eight categories, with the base and reconciliation requests, the committee marks, the enacted authorization (P.L. 119-60) and appropriation (P.L. 119-75), and Appendix Table A-1, which carries the Department's allocation of P.L. 119-21 money line by line.

  • R49023, FY2027 Defense Budget: Funding for Selected Weapon Systems, version 4, 3 September 2026. One hundred and four lines: 101 in seven category tables and 3 in Appendix Table A-1. It carries the discretionary and mandatory requests, the authorization as passed by the House (H.R. 8800), the authorization as reported by the Senate Armed Services Committee (S. 4784), and the appropriation as reported by the House Appropriations Committee (H.R. 9495). Nothing had been enacted and the Senate appropriators had not reported, so those columns are blank by design.

The two reports are not symmetrical, and the design does not pretend they are. The FY2026 report describes a year that has been enacted; the FY2027 report describes a request in the middle of its passage through Congress. The comparison between them is therefore always a comparison of requests, never of enacted outcomes.

3.5 The eight categories

Lines are grouped into the eight categories CRS uses in its own narrative: aircraft; command, control, communications, computers, and intelligence (C4I); space-based systems; ground systems; hypersonic weapons; missile defense; missiles and munitions; and shipbuilding and maritime systems. For FY2027 CRS prints communications and space-based systems in a single table but states a separate narrative total for each, so the lab carries them as two categories, which keeps the FY2027 categories aligned with the eight used for FY2026.

3.6 The pay model

The pay model prices a raise grade by grade across the whole active-duty force of 1,285,084 members in 24 pay grades. Basic pay comes from the January 2026 monthly table, 24 grades by 22 steps of years of service. Strength by grade and service comes from Defense Manpower Data Center counts as of 30 September 2025. The burden rates, meaning retired pay accrual, agency contributions to the Thrift Savings Plan, and employer payroll taxes, come from the Military Personnel Programs exhibit (the M-1) for the year being priced, so an FY2027 raise carries FY2027 burdens. Pay at the top of the officer scale is held at the statutory cap. A second layer extends basic pay to regular military compensation, and a third sets the model beside the Department's published composite standard pay rates as an independent check.

4. How Each Module Was Formed and Tested

Five analytical modules sit on top of the workbook. Each was specified with a stated test before it was trusted, and each test is written either into the workbook's quality assurance sheets or into the page's verification suite, so the page cannot display a result the checks did not pass.

4.1 The crosswalk and the bridge

Statement: The two fiscal years can be compared line by line only on lines that carry a figure in both, and that comparison must reconcile exactly to the two published totals.

How it was formed: The crosswalk is the direct answer to the vocabulary problem described in section 2.2 and the blank problem described in section 2.3. It maps the FY2026 base request to the FY2027 discretionary request, since both are what the Department asked Congress to provide through regular appropriations, and the FY2026 reconciliation request to the FY2027 mandatory request, since both are what it asked to receive outside that process. It records that the FY2026 enacted appropriation has no FY2027 counterpart, so any comparison against the House Appropriations Committee mark is indicative only. Each program match carries its basis: the same label in both reports, a match on name after removing the CRS product reference, or a named analyst decision, such as treating the Constellation-class frigate and FF(X) as the same program after CRS dropped the class name.

How it was tested: The bridge must close to zero. FY2026 total request on every line listed for that year, $171.67 billion, less $3.11 billion on lines with no FY2027 figure, gives $168.56 billion on the matched lines. FY2027 total request, $271.28 billion, less $17.69 billion on lines appearing for the first time, gives $253.59 billion. The difference, $85.03 billion, must equal the sum of the line level change column, and the generator refuses to write a page if it does not.

Result: The bridge closes with a residual of zero. Ninety-five lines are listed in both reports, but only 91 carry a figure in both; the four without an FY2027 figure are the programs named in section 2.3. Six lines are new in FY2027, carrying $17.69 billion, and two FY2026 lines do not appear in FY2027, carrying $0.90 billion. The largest movements on the matched lines are concentrated in missile defense, where the PAC-3 Missile Segment Enhancement rises from $1.36 billion to $13.96 billion in total request and Terminal High Altitude Area Defense from $1.53 billion to $12.49 billion. In both cases almost all of the increase sits in the mandatory column.

4.2 The scenario composer

Statement: An instruction written the way one would say it to a staffer should price exactly, against a stated baseline, and show where its offset comes from.

How it was formed: Budget debates are conducted in sentences, not in cell references. A proposal to add a submarine, trim a category by a tenth, or terminate a program is usually stated in words and priced loosely, if at all. The composer takes the sentence as its input. It understands add, cut, set, and zero, in dollars or in percentages, against a named system, a category, a service, or the whole portfolio, and it accepts an offset clause. The reader chooses one of five baselines (the discretionary request, the total request, the House-passed bill, the SASC-reported bill, or the HAC-reported bill) and one of four offset rules (no offset, pro rata across untouched lines, holding each category constant, or a single absorbing system).

How it was tested: The engine assertions exercise every instruction form, category and service targets, and the offset rules against a known result. A bare number with no unit is accepted but flagged as an assumed figure in billions. An instruction the parser cannot read is refused rather than guessed. On the page, a three-line script containing one deliberate nonsense line was run through the composer, and exactly that line was flagged.

Result: On the FY2027 discretionary baseline the composer holds 104 lines in scope, totaling $204.52 billion, and reports the net change, the lines moved in each direction, the change by category, and the line by line result, all of which can be downloaded as a CSV file.

4.3 The claim tester

Statement: A figure means little on its own. Any change, whether a percentage of a line or an amount in any unit, should be expressible in proportion four ways: as a share of its own line, as units of the thing it buys, as a cut somewhere else, and as an across-the-board military pay raise.

How it was formed: Public argument about defense money often turns on a single number presented without a denominator. The claim tester supplies the denominators. It also accepts a claim of the reader's own, an amount for something that appears in neither report. Such a claim has no baseline, no published quantity, and no unit cost, so those rows stay blank and say why; nothing is invented to fill them.

How it was tested: The page suite runs the tester across all four offset modes, both change types, all four amount units (dollars, thousands, millions, and billions), and a claim of the reader's own.

Result: The default example shows what the tool is for. A ten percent increase to the Virginia-class submarine line, itself 22.5 percent of the shipbuilding category's discretionary request, is $1.38 billion. That sum would buy 0.0862 of a Columbia-class boat at the $16.04 billion a unit that CRS's figures imply, or fund an across-the-board raise of about 1.45 percent. At the other end of the scale, a claim of two million dollars comes to 0.00098 percent of the $204.5 billion discretionary request across all 104 lines, the equivalent of a 0.0021 percent raise, and roughly two dollars a member.

4.4 The pay and compensation model

Statement: The cost of a raise depends on the grade that receives it, the month it takes effect, and burden rates that belong to the year being priced.

How it was formed: Pay is where budget claims most often meet the force itself, and it is where a single assumed rate most easily distorts a total. The retired pay accrual charge, for example, is a percentage of basic pay set actuarially each year. Pricing one year's raise at another year's rate misstates its cost. The model therefore derives its burden rates from the M-1 for each year rather than assuming them.

How it was tested: The engine confirms E-4 strength at 239,169 and Army E-4 strength at 95,338, that the five services sum exactly to the whole-force cost, that the FY2026 and FY2027 accrual rates differ as the M-1 says they do, and that O-10 pay is held at the statutory cap. The regular military compensation layer is tested against the M-1 housing lines, which it reproduces exactly for officers and for the enlisted force, and against an implied share of enlisted members drawing cash subsistence that is not fitted and so serves as a free diagnostic.

Result: The implied retired pay accrual rate is 24.2 percent of basic pay for FY2026, which corroborates the 24.3 percent full-time normal cost set by the DoD Board of Actuaries within rounding, and 20.1 percent for FY2027. Pricing an FY2027 raise at the FY2026 rate would overstate the accrual component by about a fifth. On FY2027 rates, one percentage point of basic pay across the whole active-duty force costs $0.95 billion fully burdened. The implied share of enlisted members drawing cash subsistence is 74.1 percent against a reasonable expectation of about four fifths, which is consistent with members on subsistence in kind drawing no cash allowance.

One sourced correction to an existing FY2026 sheet came out of this work. The Thrift Savings Plan assumption in the FY2026 pay sheet had been a pasted 1.0 percent, the automatic contribution alone, with matching omitted. It is now a live formula that adds the automatic 1 percent to the matching rate the M-1 reports for FY2026. It is the only change made to an FY2026 sheet, and it is documented where it was made.

4.5 The reconciliation cliff

Statement: Reconciliation money is one-time money. Sustaining the FY2026 program from FY2027 onward requires discretionary appropriations to carry what reconciliation carried, and the size of that requirement can be stated exactly under a stated assumption.

How it was formed: P.L. 119-21 provided $156.2 billion for defense, available for obligation through 30 September 2029, and the Department allocated almost all of it in its FY2026 spend plan. Those dollars do not repeat. The projection holds the FY2026 program constant and asks what discretionary appropriations would have to add to sustain it, before any new content. It has three settings: price growth a year, real growth in content, and the share of mandatory money assumed to be replaced.

How it was tested: At the default settings the first-year gap is $48.4296 billion and the cumulative gap through FY2031 is $252.0296 billion, both matching the workbook exactly. Setting the replacement share to 100 percent, which models Congress backfilling every reconciliation dollar with appropriations, closes the gap to zero.

Result: For the selected systems, 27.1 percent of FY2026 enacted funding, $47.48 billion of $175.08 billion, came through the reconciliation spend plan. At two percent price growth and no real growth, sustaining that program would require discretionary appropriations for these lines to rise 40.0 percent in FY2027, a gap of $48.43 billion in the first year, or about $46.6 billion after the $1.8 billion of P.L. 119-21 money that remained unallocated, and $252.03 billion over the five years through FY2031.

5. The Evidence Base: Every Source and Why It Was Used

Seventeen references feed the workbook, numbered S1 to S17 as a single list that runs across the FY2026 and FY2027 source sheets. Each was selected for a specific analytical function rather than for general relevance, and each entry in the workbook carries its title, publisher, date, what it supplies, and a link. The generator refuses to build if the two source sheets ever reuse a reference number, so the list cannot fork.

5.1 The budget reports

S1. Congressional Research Service, FY2026 Defense Budget: Funding for Selected Weapon Systems, R48860, version 7, by Daniel M. Gettinger, updated 2 July 2026. This is the backbone of the FY2026 side. It supplies every FY2026 weapon system figure, the toplines, the dated legislative actions that make up the timeline, and the reconciliation appendix from which the spend plan is read line by line.

S12. Congressional Research Service, FY2027 Defense Budget: Funding for Selected Weapon Systems, R49023, version 4, 3 September 2026. This is the backbone of the FY2027 side. It supplies Tables 1 to 7 and Appendix Table A-1 for all 104 selected lines, the narrative total for each category, the committee aggregates stated in its Summary, and the appendix on selected administration initiatives. Every FY2027 line carries the page and footnote in this report from which it was taken.

5.2 Pay and force structure sources

S2. The White House, executive order adjusting certain rates of pay, Schedule 8, Part I, 18 December 2025, effective 1 January 2026. It supplies the full 2026 monthly basic pay table and the Executive Schedule caps on military basic pay. It is used in preference to secondary pay charts because it is the legal instrument that sets the rates.

S3 and S4. Congressional Research Service, Defense Primer: Military Enlisted Personnel (IF10684) and Defense Primer: Military Officers (IF10685), both 7 January 2026, reporting Defense Manpower Data Center counts as of 30 September 2025. Together they supply active-duty strength by pay grade and by service, which is the quantity every raise is multiplied by.

S11. Navy Cyberspace, 2026 military pay chart, updated December 2025. It supplies the legislative history of the 3.8 percent January 2026 raise and serves only as a cross-check on the pay table, never as its source.

S13. Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Personnel and Readiness, Selected Military Compensation Tables, 1 January 2023, the most recent edition published. It supplies regular military compensation by grade: basic pay, the average housing and subsistence allowances, the federal income tax advantage, and the share of members drawing cash housing. It supplies the structure to which the priced year is fitted, not the level.

S14. Congressional Research Service, Defense Primer: Regular Military Compensation (IF10532), updated 2026. It supplies the definition of regular military compensation and its standing in defense compensation policy, so that the term is used as the Department uses it.

S15. Office of the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller), Military Composite Standard Pay and Reimbursement Rates, Tab K, FY2026 and FY2027 editions. It supplies average basic pay and the composite standard pay rate for every grade in every service, and is used as an independent cross-check on the model's cost per member.

S16. Office of the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller), Military Personnel Programs (M-1), FY2026 and FY2027 President's Budget. It supplies department-wide basic pay, retired pay accrual, Thrift Savings Plan matching, and the housing and subsistence allowance dollars. It sets the level to which the 2023 compensation structure is scaled, and from it the model derives the burden rates for each year rather than assuming them.

5.3 Actuarial and tax parameters

S5. Department of the Air Force, FY2026 budget estimates for the military personnel appropriation, Space Force volume, 2025. It supplies the FY2026 retired pay accrual normal cost percentages: 24.3 percent full time and 22.6 percent part time.

S6. DoD Board of Actuaries, minutes of the 15 September 2025 meeting. It confirms the FY2026 full-time normal cost percentage of 24.3 percent and the Treasury share of 33.5 percent. It is the authority against which the model's implied accrual rate is checked.

S7. DoD Office of the Actuary, Valuation of the Military Retirement Fund, as of 30 September 2023. It supplies full-time basic payroll of $74.6 billion in FY2024, used as a calibration reference for the scale of the pay model.

S8. Social Security Administration, 2026 Social Security changes fact sheet, October 2025. It supplies the employer payroll tax rates and the 2026 taxable maximum of $184,500.

S17. Internal Revenue Service, tax year 2026 inflation adjustments, 2025. It supplies the federal income tax brackets and standard deductions, single and married filing jointly, from which the marginal rate behind the tax advantage is derived.

5.4 Contextual sources

S9. Breaking Defense, reporting of 24 June 2026 on the $87.6 billion supplemental request, of which $67 billion was for defense. It supplies the composition of that request, including $12.1 billion for classified programs.

S10. The Washington Times, reporting of 13 September 2026 on the status of the supplemental. It establishes that as of mid-September 2026 the request had not been enacted.

These two are contextual rather than parametric, meaning they inform how the toplines are presented rather than supplying numbers the analysis depends on. They are the only press sources in the list, and they are used for the status of a request, which is a matter of record, not for any figure that enters a calculation.

5.5 What was deliberately not used

Every source document was checked for integrity before any figure was taken from it, because a file that looks like a report but is in fact a poor scan or a text dump will extract silently and wrongly. Each was verified to be a genuine PDF with a usable text layer. One document on program and budgeting reform had almost no text layer on the pages sampled and was not used. Duplicate copies of the same document were identified and only one of each was read.

Two further decisions of the same kind are worth naming. The R49023 narrative mentions a Drone Dominance initiative that appears in no table. Nothing was mapped to that label, because a figure that CRS did not print cannot be carried without inventing it. And the Selected Military Compensation Tables have not been published since the 1 January 2023 edition; no 2024, 2025, 2026, or 2027 edition exists, and a 2027 edition cannot exist until the FY2027 raise is set. Rather than treat 2023 dollar levels as current, the project takes only the structure from that edition and takes the level from the M-1 for the year being priced. The method and its limits are set out in the workbook where the scaling is done.

6. How the Workbook Was Built

The workbook is the project's single source of truth, and the page is only ever a reading of it. A design principle runs through all twenty-seven sheets: every computed cell is a live formula, and no result computed in Python is ever pasted into a cell. That rule is what allows a reader to audit a figure without running any code.

6.1 Conventions

Blue cells hold source data as published and are the only cells a user should edit. Black cells are formulas. Green cells are links pulled from another sheet. A pale yellow fill marks a scenario lever, meant to be changed. Currency is shown in billions of dollars to two decimal places, zeros display as a hyphen, negatives sit in parentheses, and percentages are stored as fractions. Above all of these sits one rule: a blank cell means CRS printed no figure on that line. It is not a zero and it is not a cut. The FY2026 sheets state a narrower convention in their own header notes, where formulas read a blank as zero for the purpose of summation; the FY2027 sheets and the crosswalk keep blanks blank all the way through.

6.2 The FY2026 lines

The FY2026 master sheet holds the 97 lines of R48860 in forty-seven columns, carrying every column CRS printed together with derived metrics. Category, service, and subcategory rollups, the actions of each chamber on each line, budget line cost per unit for every line carrying a quantity, and the FY2026 scenario and claim sheets are built on top of it. The service label on each FY2026 line is the workbook's own classification, not a CRS field, and the page says so.

6.3 The FY2027 lines

The FY2027 lines were extracted from R49023 with pdfplumber using coordinates rather than text order. Each right-aligned number was assigned to the column header above it by position, which preserves the blank cells that a simple text extraction would collapse, since a text stream cannot tell an empty column from a missing one. The extraction was then checked against an independent transcription read by eye from images of the pages, without reference to what the parser had produced. The two were compared by machine across 607 cell slots. Nothing was built until the count of mismatches reached zero, and it did.

Each of the 104 lines carries provenance columns recording the CRS page and footnote from which it came. Two lines, C-40 Fleet Expansion and M10 Booker, are listed by CRS with no figure in any column, and they are carried that way. Three cells printed by CRS as less than one hundredth of a billion, the Skyraider II SASC mark and the mandatory requests for the America-class and San Antonio-class ships, are carried as 0.00 with the printed text recorded beside them.

6.4 The crosswalk

The year comparison sheet joins the two years in 103 rows: 95 lines listed in both reports, 6 new in FY2027, and 2 present only in FY2026. It carries the bridge that reconciles the two totals with the change column and a separate list of the lines present in one year only. The column mapping described in section 4.1 is stated at the head of the sheet, and every row records the basis on which its two years were matched.

6.5 Toplines, reconciliation, and the timeline

A reconciliation sheet sets the FY2026 reconciliation request against the Department's spend plan, including Appendix Table A-1 of R48860 line by line. A topline sheet places the selected systems inside the Department's whole request, alongside the reconciliation law and the pending supplemental. A timeline sheet records each dated legislative action on the FY2026 request, from the submission of the budget in May 2025 to the publication of the report version used here, with each event's distance in days from the start of the fiscal year and the page of R48860 on which it is recorded. The projection sheet carries the reconciliation cliff from FY2027 to FY2031.

6.6 Pay and force structure

The January 2026 pay table and the force structure sheet supply the two inputs every pay calculation needs. The FY2026 pay sheet prices a raise for that year, including its cost by service. The two year pay module derives the burden rates from the M-1, holds the assumptions, and carries the 24 grade cost table. The allowances sheet builds regular military compensation per grade and reconciles the housing dollars to the M-1. The composite sheet holds the Department's composite standard rates for each grade and service in both editions. It is sparse by design: where a service published no rate for a grade the cell is blank, and the spread against the model reads "not printed".

6.7 The quality assurance sheets

Two sheets hold the checks. The FY2026 sheet ties each category's computed figures to the totals CRS states in its narrative, with the page on which each narrative figure appears, and runs a set of structural checks. The FY2027 sheet records every check behind the extension in eight lettered sections, from line counts and transcription agreement through category totals, committee aggregates, source contradictions, and the pay derivations. A glossary and field dictionary and the two source sheets complete the workbook.

7. Repair, Reconciliation, and Verification

7.1 Auditing the cached results

The page reads the workbook's cached formula results, because openpyxl cannot evaluate formulas. Before anything was built, those cached results were audited, and the workbook as it then stood did not hold up. Twenty-four cells in the FY2026 pay sheet had cached the error value #VALUE!, and eight cells elsewhere had cached a zero where the formula did not produce one. The consequence was visible on the year comparison sheet, where the bridge residual read $85.03 billion when it should have read zero, so that the sheet reported its own reconciliation as failing. These were stale caches, not live errors. Recalculating the workbook produced correct figures with no change to any formula.

Eight further cells called a function that does not exist. They had carried a stale cached string, so the fault stayed hidden until recalculation, at which point all eight returned an error. Each is now the plain string concatenation it was meant to be. After the repair the workbook carries no formula errors in any of its twenty-seven sheets, and every difference from a clean recalculation of the unrepaired original is accounted for.

7.2 The blank that became a zero

The more consequential defect was methodological. The year comparison sheet pulled its figures across from other sheets with bare references. A bare reference to an empty cell returns zero in Excel. The change columns beside those pulls are guarded to stay blank whenever a line carries a figure in only one year, but the guard tests for an empty value, and a zero is not empty. LibreOffice carries emptiness through a bare reference, so the guards fired there and the fault was invisible; Excel does not, so in Excel the four programs R49023 lists without an FY2027 figure would have read as cuts of their entire FY2026 request.

Each of the 1,099 pulls on that sheet is now wrapped so that a blank survives the hop between sheets in either engine. The repair changed 143 displayed cells from a phantom $0.00 to a blank and changed nothing else. The FY2026 sheets were deliberately left alone, because the FY2026 master sheet documents its own convention that formulas read a blank as zero for summation, and wrapping those pulls would have altered documented behavior rather than repaired a fault.

7.3 Tie-outs against the CRS narrative

CRS rounds each line to the hundredth of a billion and states each narrative total to the tenth, so a gap of a few hundredths between a computed total and a stated one is rounding and is shown as such. For FY2026, 53 tie-outs pass, 21 structural checks pass, and 3 are marked for review because the CRS narrative disagrees with the CRS tables by more than rounding.

  • Ground systems, enacted authorization: the tables give $4.32 billion against $4.7 billion in the narrative. Table 3 prints no enacted authorization figure for the Next Generation Squad Weapon, and adding that line at its request level of $0.39 billion reproduces the narrative figure, so the difference is a gap in the published table rather than an error in the workbook. The cell is left blank; the implied figure is not written into it.

  • Missiles and munitions, reconciliation request: the table lines sum to $7.63 billion against $7.5 billion in the narrative. CRS's own components imply about $7.6 billion, and Stinger's $0.08 billion reconciliation request never appears in Appendix Table A-1. The narrative figure appears to be rounded down.

  • Shipbuilding and maritime, total request: the narrative says $48.1 billion, while CRS's own components, $21.8 billion in base and $27.0 billion in reconciliation, sum to $48.8 billion, which is what the workbook computes. The narrative figure appears to be a typographical error.

For FY2027, every one of the eight computed category totals falls within CRS rounding of its narrative figure, and the sum of the eight, $271.28 billion, sits two hundredths below the $271.3 billion CRS states.

7.4 Contradictions left standing

Some disagreements inside the sources cannot be resolved from the sources, and the project does not resolve them. The largest concerns the Senate Armed Services Committee. The R49023 Summary says that committee proposed authorizing the amount requested. The committee's own column in the same report departs from the request on 27 lines and nets to about $0.67 billion above it. The largest departures are the Arleigh Burke-class destroyer at plus $2.60 billion, the E-7A Wedgetail at plus $1.50 billion, and the Family of Affordable Mass Missile at plus $0.85 billion, against the aircraft carrier refueling complex overhaul at minus $2.40 billion, support ships at minus $2.10 billion, and the BBG(X) battleship at minus $1.00 billion. CRS does acknowledge, in the same sentence, that both bills departed from the request for certain systems. Both figures are kept, the check is marked, and neither is adjusted. By contrast, the House and House Appropriations Committee aggregates in the Summary, plus $1.7 billion and minus $3.2 billion, agree with the tables exactly.

The remaining contradictions are smaller but handled the same way.

  • The Aegis reconciliation request is $0.45 billion in R48860 Table 5 and $0.41 billion in its Appendix Table A-1. Both are stored.

  • The House appropriations bill for FY2026, H.R. 4016, was marked up before the Department released line item detail, so that column reflects FY2025 amounts and projections. Comparisons against it are marked indicative.

  • Appendix Table A-1 of R49023 prints its mandatory figures to one decimal place while its own notes say figures are rounded to the hundredth. The figures are carried exactly as printed; none was padded to two decimals.

  • The FY2026 composite rate document states Executive Schedule Level II as $225,700 for the fiscal year, while the executive order sets $228,000 a year from January 2026. Both are recorded; the pay module uses the executive order rate, which gives the $19,000 monthly cap on basic pay.

  • In the published 2023 compensation tables, the W-5 row prints the housing rate without dependents although 92 percent of W-5s are married, while every other grade is weighted by dependency status. It is carried as published, which makes the W-5 figure conservative. On five rows of the same tables, Social Security tax plus federal income tax does not equal the printed total tax. Those columns are not used, and every column that is used reconciles on all 24 rows.

7.5 The verification suite

Verification runs at four levels, and each is reported as a count rather than asserted.

  • At build time, the generator verifies 513 sheet and header cells before it writes anything. No column is ever read by position without the label beside it being checked first. If a sheet is missing or a header has moved or been reworded, the generator names the sheet and the cell and refuses to write a page.

  • The page's own JavaScript engine, run in Node, passes 124 of 124 assertions with no script errors. Headline totals are re-summed from individual lines rather than read from total rows.

  • The page, driven in a simulated browser, completes 175 interactions and 174 checks with no failures: every panel renders, every tab opens, every column sorts both ways without losing a row, and a shared link restores the year and the open tab. The resolved fonts are confirmed separately in Chromium, because the simulated browser does not resolve style variables.

  • Nine deliberate breakages of the workbook produce nine correct and specific refusals, described in section 10. An independent script re-read the workbook and compared 269 values sampled from the page's payload against the cells they came from: 269 matched.

The self-containment checks are equally explicit. The page carries no external asset reference, no import, and no network call of any kind, and Chromium loading it from a local file made zero network requests. It contains no em dashes or en dashes; its only characters outside plain ASCII are written as HTML entities. The copy of the workbook carried inside it decodes byte-identical to the source file, 416,920 bytes with the same SHA-256 digest, and reopens with all twenty-seven sheets intact. Four hyperlinks point off the page, to the author's site, profile, and email and to the Creative Commons license deed, and the check distinguishes those ordinary links, which a browser follows only when a reader clicks, from asset references, which it would fetch in order to render.

7.6 A presentation defect worth documenting

During verification, a defect was found in how the page presented the crosswalk. The workbook labels 95 lines as present in both years, but only 91 of them carry a figure in both. The bridge uses the stricter test and nets $168.56 billion on the matched FY2026 lines; the looser reading would give $170.77 billion. The page originally showed the looser figure beside the bridge's stricter one, which put two inconsistent numbers on the same screen. It now shows both counts, names the four lines responsible, and uses the bridge's definition for every change it reports.

This is recorded here rather than quietly corrected because the project's central claim is that every figure traces to a defined quantity. A page that displays two figures for what looks like the same quantity undermines that claim, even when each figure is correct on its own definition.

8. A Breakdown of Each Part of the Page

The page is organized into eight tabs, with a year switch that shows FY2026, FY2027, or both. Every chart is hand-written inline SVG, with no charting library. Each part is described here in terms of what it shows, how to read it, and why it is in the build.

8.1 Overview

The opening panel states the FY2027 position in one paragraph: $271.3 billion requested across the 101 lines inside the category tables, of which $68.2 billion is mandatory money, three committees reported, and nothing enacted, so every enacted column is blank. Below it, the category table sets the discretionary figure computed from the lines beside the figure CRS states in its narrative, with the gap shown rather than closed, followed by the mandatory request, the total, and the House, SASC, and HAC marks, with a chart of the committee marks against the discretionary request. A second chart ranks the largest lines by total request; the fourteen largest carry 60.0 percent of everything CRS tabulates for FY2027. A third counts what the House appropriators did line by line: 50 lines cut, 30 as requested, 19 increased, 4 not reported, and 1 added that was not requested. A context table places the selected systems inside the Department's whole request of $1.45 trillion, of which $1.1 trillion is discretionary and $350 billion mandatory, beside $413.1 billion for procurement and $343.7 billion for research, development, test, and evaluation. The sum of those last two, $756.8 billion, is marked as computed, because CRS states the components but never prints their total.

8.2 Scenario composer

A text box that accepts one instruction a line, such as adding two billion dollars to the Virginia-class line for a third boat, cutting missile defense by ten percent with an offset across the rest of the portfolio, or zeroing out the Sentinel. Beside it sit the choice of baseline, the offset rule, the absorbing system when one is needed, and an across-the-board adjustment. Pressing the price button returns the baseline, the total after the instructions, the net change, the number of lines moved up and down, a chart of the net change by category, and a line by line table stating the source of every change. This panel exists because a reader who wants to test a proposal should not need a spreadsheet to do it, and because a proposal priced without its offset tells only half of its story.

8.3 Claim tester

A single change, expressed four ways. The reader chooses a line or a claim of their own, a baseline, a change as a percentage or as an amount in any unit, an offset, and a line to compare against. The panel returns the change in dollars, the line before and after, the change as a share of its own line, the across-the-board raise the same money would fund on FY2027 rates, the budget line cost per unit, the units that sum would buy of the comparison line, and a one sentence reading of the whole. The budget line cost per unit is labeled for what it is, the whole budget line divided by the quantity CRS printed, which is not a flyaway cost, and the unit counts are marked as indicative because a budget line does not scale linearly with quantity.

8.4 Pay and compensation

Controls for the fiscal year priced, the service scope (the whole force or any one of five services), an across-the-board raise, an additional raise to a single grade, the month the raise takes effect, and whether retired pay accrual is included. The panel returns the full-year burdened cost, the cost in the first fiscal year, the number of members receiving a raise, the average increase in basic pay a member, a chart of burdened cost by grade, and a grade by grade table. The default example prices a ten percent raise to E-4, the largest grade at 239,169 members, from January: $1.31 billion for a full year and $0.98 billion in the first fiscal year, which covers nine of its twelve months, for an average basic pay increase of $4,179 a member before burdens.

Two further tables complete the panel. The first builds regular military compensation for each of the 24 grades from basic pay, the average housing allowance, the subsistence allowance, and the tax advantage, beside the published 2023 figure. Weighted by strength, basic pay is 54.2 percent of regular military compensation in the priced year, against 56.0 percent in the published 2023 tables. The second sets the model's burdened cost a member beside the composite standard rate for each grade, shown for the Army in the FY2027 edition. Weighted by strength, the model gives $74,147 against a composite rate of $123,895, so the model sits 40.2 percent below it, and the table states the reason on every row: the composite rate carries allowances, special pays, and permanent change of station costs that the model leaves out.

8.5 Projection

The reconciliation cliff, with three sliders: price growth a year, real growth in content, and the share of mandatory money replaced by appropriations. The panel reports the gap in FY2027, the cumulative gap through FY2031, the FY2026 mandatory dependence, and the unallocated P.L. 119-21 balance available to offset the first year, followed by a chart and a table of the sustainment gap by category and year. The text above the sliders states that the panel is arithmetic on a stated assumption and not a forecast, which is the distinction drawn in section 3.3.

8.6 Year on year

The crosswalk made visible. The panel opens by stating the column mapping in words before any figure is compared, then gives the counts: 95 lines listed in both reports, 91 carrying a figure in both, a change of $85.0 billion on the lines that can be differenced, and six new lines and two dropped. It names the four programs that stay on the spine and stay blank, charts the largest movements, sets out the bridge step by step with its zero residual, lists the lines present in one year only, and closes with the full joined spine, searchable and filterable, with the crosswalk basis stated on every row.

8.7 The data

Every line CRS printed for FY2027, as published in R49023 version 4, in one searchable table that can be filtered by category and service and downloaded as a CSV file. Each line carries an identifier, its category and service, the discretionary, mandatory, and total requests, the three committee marks, the HAC mark against the request, the budget line cost per unit where CRS printed a quantity, an enacted column reading "pending", and the CRS page. Wherever CRS printed nothing, the cell reads "not printed". This panel exists so that a reader who doubts any figure elsewhere on the page can find the line it came from without leaving the page.

8.8 Method and sources

The page's account of itself, under the heading "How this was built, and where it disagrees with itself." It carries the FY2026 tie-outs with the page of each narrative figure and an explanation for each review, the checks on the FY2027 extension in their lettered sections, the contradictions in the sources left standing, the full source list from S1 to S17, a glossary, and the legislative timeline. It is the longest panel on the page by some distance, which is deliberate: a reader should be able to see how the page was built without leaving it.

8.9 The workbook that travels with the page

Not a chart, but the page's most unusual element. The workbook is carried inside the page as encoded data, and the Workbook button at the top decodes it and saves it. Decoding happens only when the button is pressed, so it costs nothing until it is wanted. The page grew from about 455 kilobytes to about one megabyte to carry it. The design intent is that the page and its source cannot be separated: whoever holds the page holds every formula behind it.

9. Limitations Carried Openly

The project treats its limitations as part of the deliverable rather than as an appendix. Each of the following is stated on the page or in the workbook where it applies.

  • The reports cover selected weapon systems, not the defense budget. The 101 FY2027 lines in the category tables carry $271.28 billion against a Department request of $1.45 trillion. Every figure is budget authority, not outlays, and outlays lag budget authority heavily for shipbuilding and procurement, so no table here shows the economic timing of defense spending.

  • Nothing in FY2027 has been enacted. The enacted authorization, enacted appropriation, and Senate appropriations columns are empty by design, and the nearest FY2027 counterpart to an enacted FY2026 figure is a committee mark, so any comparison against it is indicative.

  • R49023 has no service field. FY2027 lines inherit a service label through the crosswalk where they pair with an FY2026 line, and are left blank otherwise rather than guessed. Six FY2027 lines therefore read "not classified" and fall outside any service filter.

  • Basic pay is the January 2026 table in both years. Pricing FY2027 switches the burden rates, the caps, and the composite comparison, but not basic pay itself, because no FY2027 pay table can exist until the FY2027 raise is set. Regular military compensation takes its structure from 2023 tables, the newest published, and its level from the M-1.

  • Budget line cost per unit is not flyaway cost, and a budget line is not linear in quantity. Unit counts in the claim tester are indicative only.

  • An instruction that moves every line in a category leaves the rule that holds each category constant with no pool to draw on, so part of the net stays on the topline. The page states this in words rather than showing an unexplained residual.

  • A claim of the reader's own has no baseline, no published quantity, and no unit cost. Those rows stay blank, and the page says why.

  • The projection holds the FY2026 program constant. It prices sustainment of what was bought, not the program the Department proposes, and it assumes nothing about what Congress will do.

10. Refusal Conditions and Update Triggers

A tool someone can rely on is a tool that names, in advance, the conditions under which it will stop. The generator was tested against nine deliberate breakages, and each produced a specific refusal that named the sheet and cell at fault and wrote no page.

  • A reworded header, a removed sheet, or a header shifted by one column.

  • An altered tie-out, a bridge that stops closing, or a deleted line.

  • A quality assurance check switched to FAIL.

  • An FY2027 enacted column filled in while the build still asserts that nothing has been enacted.

  • A workbook that was rewritten and never recalculated, so that its cached results are stale.

Two further refusals guard the publication itself: the generator will not build if the two source sheets reuse a reference number, and it will not write a page that carries any asset reference pointing off the file.

Several events outside the project will change its figures, and each is a trigger for a rebuild rather than a reason for the page to be silently out of date.

  • Enactment of the FY2027 authorization or appropriation, or a report by the Senate Appropriations Committee. The empty columns will then be populated, and the generator's assertion that they are empty will be relaxed deliberately rather than removed.

  • A new version of R49023. CRS revises these reports as Congress acts, and each revision will be transcribed by the same double procedure and compared at the cell level before anything is rebuilt.

  • Setting of the FY2027 pay raise, which makes an FY2027 basic pay table possible, and any new edition of the Selected Military Compensation Tables.

  • Enactment of the June 2026 supplemental request, which would change the toplines against which the selected systems are read.

11. Why This Matters for United States Defense Policy

11.1 The fiscal implication

For the selected weapon systems, more than a quarter of FY2026 enacted funding came through a single reconciliation law. In shipbuilding the share was 45.6 percent, and in missiles and munitions 31.1 percent. That money was allocated once, and it does not recur. Holding the FY2026 program constant from FY2027 onward would require discretionary appropriations for these lines to rise 40.0 percent in a single year, and in shipbuilding by 87.5 percent. The arithmetic does not say that Congress will fail to find the money. It says how much money there is to find, and where the gap is deepest.

The FY2027 request deepens the question rather than answering it. It asks for $68.16 billion in mandatory funding inside the category tables and a further $100.6 billion for the three initiatives in Appendix Table A-1, none of it enacted and with no reconciliation law behind it. Missile defense is the clearest case: $27.78 billion of its $43.51 billion, nearly two thirds, is requested as mandatory money, and the two largest increases between the years, the PAC-3 Missile Segment Enhancement and Terminal High Altitude Area Defense, are carried almost entirely by the mandatory column. Whether those programs grow as requested therefore depends less on the ordinary appropriations process than on whether Congress legislates mandatory money again. That is a question about the durability of a funding mechanism, and it is one the enacted totals alone would conceal. For any assessment of whether a program, a doctrine, or a weapon can realistically be carried through, this is the first question to ask of its funding: not whether it is funded this year, but whether the mechanism that funds it will recur.

11.2 The oversight implication

Budget tables are read far more often than they are read carefully, and the errors that follow are not random. They run in the direction of manufactured cuts. A blank read as a zero terminates a program no one terminated. A committee's stated aggregate, taken on trust, can differ from what its own column contains, as the SASC summary does by $0.67 billion. And a year on year comparison that does not fix its columns first can say almost anything.

The F-35 shows how much depends on that last point. Its FY2026 base request was $12.12 billion and its FY2027 discretionary request is $9.07 billion, a fall of about a quarter. Its total request rose from $13.12 billion to $21.38 billion, an increase of nearly two thirds, because $12.31 billion of the FY2027 request is mandatory. Whether the F-35 request was cut or grown depends entirely on which column is read. For anyone conducting oversight, briefing a member, or reporting on the defense budget, the practical lesson is that a comparison is only as sound as the mapping stated before it.

11.3 The personnel implication

Military pay is among the most frequently argued subjects in the defense budget, and among the most frequently mispriced. The cost of a raise depends on burden rates that change from year to year: the implied retired pay accrual rate fell from 24.2 percent of basic pay in FY2026 to 20.1 percent in FY2027, so a raise priced at the earlier rate overstates its accrual cost by about a fifth. On FY2027 rates, one percentage point of basic pay across the active-duty force costs $0.95 billion fully burdened, which gives any weapon system figure a ready translation into terms of the force.

Two further distinctions matter for policy. A raise to basic pay does not move the allowances, so a basic pay raise of a given percentage is a smaller percentage increase in regular military compensation, of which basic pay is only about 54 percent. And the burdened cost of basic pay alone sits about 40 percent below the Department's composite standard rate, which is a fuller cost per member because it includes allowances, special pays, and moving costs. Using the composite rate to price a raise to basic pay would overstate the cost substantially. A claim about the cost of paying the force should state which of these quantities it means.

11.4 The methodological implication

There is a final contribution that is not about any single program. The project demonstrates that a public budget document can be carried into an analytical instrument without losing its provenance: every figure a live formula, every line tied to its page and footnote, every total set beside the one the source states, every contradiction kept rather than reconciled, and a build that refuses to run when any of that breaks. The three narrative figures that disagree with CRS's own tables, and the SASC summary that disagrees with the SASC column, are published as findings rather than buried.

Defense budget analysis frequently circulates as a single clean number with no path back to its source. This project's position is that a figure carried with its provenance, and with its disagreements stated, is more useful to a decision maker than a tidier total that has been silently reconciled.

12. Technical Architecture and Reproduction

The project has three parts: the workbook, the generator, and the page. The workbook, data/FY2026_FY2027_Defense_Budget_Analytics.xlsx, carries twenty-seven sheets and more than fifteen thousand live formulas. The generator, src/fy2627_budget_lab.py, is written in Python 3 and depends only on openpyxl. It reads the workbook's cached formula results, verifies 513 sheet and header cells, and writes the page. Because openpyxl cannot evaluate formulas, a workbook rewritten by any script must be reopened and recalculated before the generator will read it, and the generator refuses with an explicit instruction if it has not been.

The page, index.html, is a single file of about one megabyte with one script element and one style element, everything inline, and no content delivery network, web font, charting library, or framework. Navigation, buttons, labels, and table headings are set in Segoe UI with Calibri and system sans-serif fonts behind it; everything a reader reads is set in Times New Roman; figures are monospaced. Neither face is a web font, so nothing is fetched. Motion is soft and staged, and all of it, including the animation delays, is switched off for readers who ask their system to reduce motion. The page has a light and a dark theme, and a shared link restores the year and the open tab.

Reproduction is a single command run from the repository root:

python3 src/fy2627_budget_lab.py --xlsx data/FY2026_FY2027_Defense_Budget_Analytics.xlsx --out index.html --icons assets/icons

Two GitHub Actions workflows accompany the repository. The first rebuilds the page from the workbook on every push and pull request and fails if the result differs from the committed page, with only the build date normalized before the comparison, and then runs a self-containment check that fails if the page has picked up an external asset reference, an em dash, or an en dash, or has lost the workbook it carries. The second publishes the repository to GitHub Pages. Neither needs a secret, and neither can write to the repository. The repository also carries a build and verification note, a sheet by sheet data dictionary, a citation file, and this report.

13. What Remains

Three areas of work are outstanding.

The first is enactment. The FY2027 authorization and appropriation have not been enacted, and the Senate appropriators had not reported when the page was built. When they act, the empty columns will be populated, the generator's assertion that they are empty will be relaxed deliberately, and the comparison between the two years can move from requests to enacted outcomes. Until then, every FY2027 figure on the page is a request or a committee proposal.

The second is pay. No FY2027 basic pay table can exist until the FY2027 raise is set, and the compensation tables that supply the structure of regular military compensation have not been published since 2023. When either appears, the pay model will be repriced on it, and the scaling that currently bridges the 2023 structure to the M-1 level can be tested against a published figure rather than carried as a method.

The third is classification. R49023 carries no service field, and six FY2027 lines that have no FY2026 counterpart read "not classified". Assigning them a service would require a documented analyst decision for each, of the kind the crosswalk already records, rather than a guess, and until that work is done they are left as they are.
